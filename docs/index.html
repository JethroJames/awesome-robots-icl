Abstract

Paper details & citation ↗

arXiv:2609.36012

General-purpose robots must infer what a new task requires and translate that understanding into appropriate physical action. In-context learning (ICL) for robots supports this process by using demonstrations and interaction to direct existing competence with neural parameters held fixed during deployment. We organize this literature review around the interfaces connecting contextual evidence to execution, distinguishing four families: context-conditioned policies, geometric demonstration transfer, world-model-based control, and skill- and agent-based execution. Comparing these interfaces clarifies their transfer assumptions and the roles of training, correspondence, and memory in making context useful. Across manipulation and navigation, we examine how these mechanisms preserve taught requirements as objects, environments, and execution conditions change. This analysis links method design to evaluation practices that distinguish responsiveness to teaching, physical transfer, and benefits from retained experience. The resulting agenda connects compositional task acquisition and faithful transfer with physical recursive self-improvement, in which experience improves the ability to learn subsequent tasks.

Understanding Robot ICL

What is in-context learning for robots?

A deployed robot uses demonstrations, instructions, interaction history, or feedback to decide how to act in the present task. In the survey’s central setting, neural parameters stay fixed: new evidence changes contextual state and decisions. For example, a demonstration may teach an assembly order, while contact history reveals how tightly a part fits. Prior perception and control make these requirements executable.

Foundations · Section 2 ↗

How does Robot ICL differ from fine-tuning?

Fine-tuning stores task evidence in updated neural weights. Robot ICL supplies evidence during inference, through a demonstration, memory, a geometric reference, or an executable procedure. Both rely on prior competence. They differ in where adaptation is stored, how quickly new evidence can be used, and what persists after a reset. The survey discusses parameter adaptation as a comparison and distinguishes it from fixed-parameter context use.

Adaptation regimes · Section 2 ↗

How should Robot ICL be evaluated?

Task success alone cannot show which information the robot learned from context. Change the demonstration while holding the scene fixed to test responsiveness to teaching; change objects, scenes, or dynamics to test transfer; then compare a later attempt with and without retained experience. Report the execution conditions, teaching effort, response delay, and reuse cost alongside success. These comparisons connect the inferred requirement to physical behavior.

How context shapes action.

01

Context-conditioned policies

Action inference

Context specifies an action distribution or retrieves action evidence for the current observation. Demonstrations can preserve task order, object relations, and interaction history. Transfer depends on whether the learned representation retains the taught requirement and links it to the receiving robot’s controls.

02

Geometric demonstration transfer

Motion references

Context establishes poses, contacts, or a motion reference through spatial correspondence and retargeting. A controller then tracks that reference. Geometric structure makes the transfer explicit, while perception errors, occlusion, and changed contact conditions can invalidate the alignment.

03

World-model-based control

Predicted futures

Context helps construct a future observation, trajectory, or simulated outcome. Planning or an action decoder converts that intermediate into control. A plausible future must also be physically achievable, and prediction errors matter when they change the selected action.

04

Skill- and agent-based execution

Executable procedures

Context selects or composes skills, programs, and tool calls. Execution feedback can verify a step and guide recovery. Transfer depends on correctly grounding instructions, meeting skill preconditions, and retaining guidance that remains applicable to later tasks.

Context for navigation.

Navigation makes different evidence available: a demonstrated route, earlier observations of the environment, examples of decisions, or feedback about outcomes. The survey compares what each source resolves when the destination or relevant state is outside the current view.

What does the robot learn
from context?

Physical recursive self-improvement asks whether experience improves a robot’s ability to learn subsequent tasks. The survey connects this objective to contextual reuse, correction, and the conditions under which a lesson remains useful.

A new task.

Identify which order, relation, or procedure is learned from teaching, and which motor skills were already available.

A new situation.

Preserve the taught requirement while adapting its physical realization to different objects, scenes, bodies, or dynamics.

The next task.

Test whether retained experience improves subsequent learning. Distinguish contextual reuse, revised procedures, and neural updates.
